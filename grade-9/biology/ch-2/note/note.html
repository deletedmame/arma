Unit 2: Characteristics & Classification of Organisms

Based on textbook pages 19-48

2.1 Characteristics of Living Things

What makes something "alive"? Biologists recognize life by common characteristics:

  • Cellular Organization: All living things are composed of one or more cells (unicellular like bacteria, or multicellular like plants).
  • Energy Requirement: Organisms need energy for metabolic activities. Autotrophs (plants) make their own food; Heterotrophs (animals) consume others.
  • Response to Stimuli: Organisms detect and respond to changes in their internal or external environment.
  • Growth: Permanent increase in size and mass due to cell division or enlargement.
  • Reproduction: Production of offspring (sexually or asexually).
  • Excretion: Removal of metabolic wastes (e.g., carbon dioxide).
  • Ordered Complexity: High level of organization from atoms to molecules to cells.
  • Homeostasis: Maintenance of stable internal conditions (e.g., body temperature).
  • Evolutionary Adaptation: Populations evolve over time to suit their environment.

2.2 Taxonomy of Living Things

2.2.1 Principles of Classification

Taxonomy is the science of naming, identifying, and classifying organisms. Taxonomists group organisms based on similarities in structure, genetics, and evolutionary relationships.

Why classify? It makes studying the vast diversity of life manageable and reveals evolutionary links.

2.2.2 Taxonomic Hierarchies

Carolus Linnaeus (1707-1778), the "Father of Taxonomy," introduced the hierarchical system:

  1. Domain (Broadest: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya)
  2. Kingdom
  3. Phylum (or Division for plants)
  4. Class
  5. Order
  6. Family
  7. Genus
  8. Species (Most specific: group of organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring)

2.3 Relevance of Classification

Classification provides a framework to study relationships between living and extinct organisms. It has practical applications in:

  • Agriculture & Forestry: Identifying useful plant varieties.
  • Medicine: Discovering new sources of drugs (e.g., related plant species may share medicinal properties).

2.4 Linnaean System of Nomenclature

Binomial Nomenclature: The two-name system (Genus + species).

  • Rules: Genus is capitalized, species is lowercase. Both are italicized in print or underlined when handwritten.
  • Examples: Humans: Homo sapiens; Lion: Panthera leo; Maize: Zea mays.
  • Purpose: Avoids confusion caused by local names (e.g., "Bekkolo" vs. "Corn").

2.5 Common Ethiopian Animals and Plants

Ethiopia is a biodiversity hotspot with many endemic species.

  • Endemic Animals: Ethiopian Wolf (Canis simensis), Walia Ibex (Capra walie), Gelada Baboon (Theropithecus gelada).
  • Endemic Plants: Tef (Eragrostis tef), Noug (Guizotia abyssinica), Enset (Ensete ventricosum).

Dichotomous Keys

A tool used to identify unknown organisms by making a series of choices between two contrasting characteristics (e.g., "Is it warm-blooded or cold-blooded?").

2.6 The Five-Kingdom System

Proposed by Whittaker (1969) to address limitations of the two-kingdom (Plant/Animal) system. Based on cell structure, nutrition, and organization.

Kingdom Monera

  • Prokaryotic (no nucleus). Includes Eubacteria (true bacteria) and Archaebacteria (extremophiles). Some are autotrophs, others heterotrophs.

Kingdom Protista

  • Eukaryotic, mostly unicellular. Includes protozoans (animal-like), algae (plant-like), and slime molds (fungus-like).

Kingdom Fungi

  • Eukaryotic, heterotrophic (absorb nutrients). Cell walls made of chitin. Includes yeasts, molds, and mushrooms. Reproduce via spores.

Kingdom Plantae

  • Multicellular eukaryotes, autotrophic (photosynthesis). Cell walls of cellulose. Divided into Bryophytes, Pteridophytes, Gymnosperms, and Angiosperms.

Kingdom Animalia

  • Multicellular eukaryotes, heterotrophic (ingest food). Lack cell walls. Divided into Invertebrates (no backbone) and Vertebrates (backbone).

2.7 Renowned Taxonomists in Ethiopia

Despite limited resources, Ethiopia has produced distinguished taxonomists:

  • Prof. Sebsebe Demissew: Renowned for work on the Flora of Ethiopia and Eritrea.
  • Dr. Mesfin Tadesse: Expert in plant systematics.
  • Prof. Ensermu Kelbessa: Contributions to plant taxonomy.
  • Prof. Abebe Getahun: Contributions to freshwater fish taxonomy.
